## 1. Resolution mechanics
- **YES** requires the FOMC statement released at the end of the December 2026 meeting to announce a lower federal funds target range. The meeting is scheduled for **Dec 8–9, 2026**, so the statement should come out Dec 9 (Yahoo Finance, Oct 30, 2026). The question closes Dec 10.
- **Current range: 3.75%–4.00%**, with interest on reserve balances (IORB) at 3.90% from Oct 29, 2026 (Federal Reserve FOMC statement, Oct 28, 2026). Any announced cut of any size resolves YES.
- **NO** covers a hold, a hike, or anything else.
- **Edge cases:**
  - An emergency cut between meetings would not count by itself. Only the December statement counts.
  - If the meeting is moved, it is unclear whether "December 2026 meeting" still applies; the wording implies the scheduled December meeting.
  - A technical change to IORB alone, with the target range unchanged, would not count.
  - The decision is the FOMC's, as shown in its published statement.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates
1. **How soon the Fed has cut after the last hike of a cycle.** These are approximate dates of the last hike and first cut since 1994, from my own knowledge of Fed history:
   - Feb 1995 → Jul 1995: about 5 months
   - Mar 1997 → Sep 1998: about 18 months
   - May 2000 → Jan 2001: about 7.5 months
   - Jun 2006 → Sep 2007: about 15 months
   - Dec 2018 → Jul 2019: about 7 months
   - Jul 2023 → Sep 2024: about 14 months

   **No cut came within 3 months of a hike in 0 of 6 cycles.** The shortest gap was about 5 months. The most recent hike was **Sep 16, 2026**, and Dec 9 is less than 3 months later.

2. **Unconditional share of scheduled meetings with a cut since 1994.** I roughly counted about 40 cuts, including some between meetings, across about 255 scheduled meetings. That is about 15%. Most of those cuts came in easing cycles that had already started (2001, 2007–08, 2019, 2020, 2024–25).

3. **How often the Fed moves against its own latest dots.** In September 2026 the median dot implied **one more hike in 2026**. Reports say 16 of 18 participants expected at least one more hike by year-end (Raisin / Tutorsbot / FXNX, Sept 2026; the exact numbers differ by source).
   - The closest precedents for a quick reversal from a tightening bias are 2019 and 2001. In early 2019 the Fed moved from expected hikes to a pause in about one month, but the first cut took another 6 months. In Jan 2001 a cut between meetings followed a tightening bias held through Nov 2000, during a sharp growth and equity shock.
   - Swings of this kind straight from a hike bias to a cut at the next one or two meetings happen in my judgment well under 5% of the time, and almost only in crises (2001, 2008, 2020).

## 4. Drivers and scenarios
- **NO (dominant):**
  - **Hike:** the baseline for most forecasters, if inflation stays near 3.5% or higher and growth is "strong."
  - **Hold:** if the October jobs and CPI reports are mixed, or if Warsh wants more data.
  - Either outcome is NO.
- **YES paths. Each needs a sharp reversal within about 5 weeks:**
  1. **Labor market breaks down.** Two weak jobs reports (Oct and Nov) with negative payrolls and unemployment at 4.5% or higher, signaling recession. Even then, history suggests a pause comes before a cut.
  2. **Financial-stability or market shock** (credit event, equity crash, Treasury market dysfunction) that Warsh would treat as "exigent circumstances."
  3. **Energy shock reverses.** The Iran war ends or a ceasefire collapses oil prices, headline inflation falls quickly, and the Fed treats the September hike as an overreaction. This is more likely to produce a hold than a cut.
  4. **Political pressure.** The White House pushes for cuts. The current committee, with hawkish dissents and hawkish reserve-bank presidents, looks resistant to this.
- **What YES depends on:** a committee that dissented for a hike in October would need to swing to a cut majority. Warsh would have to lead that shift, without his usual forward guidance.
