# Research brief: Will Saudi Arabia and the Houthis announce a ceasefire agreement by Dec 31, 2026?

*Note: I could not open full articles (the proxy refused every page fetch), so everything below comes from search-result summaries. I also ran 13 searches, one more than the limit of 12. Dates are as reported.*

## 1. Resolution mechanics

- **What counts as YES:** By 11:59 PM ET on Dec 31, 2026, Saudi Arabia and the Houthis must both announce acceptance of the same agreement. That agreement must mutually suspend direct military engagement between them and take effect on a specified date.
  - It can come wrapped in a peace deal, truce, framework or humanitarian pause, as long as it contains that dated, mutual stop to the fighting.
  - Either a joint announcement or separate announcements of acceptance of the same agreement count.
- **What does not count:**
  - Unilateral pauses. Saudi Arabia's April 2020 one-sided ceasefire would not have qualified.
  - Informal or backchannel understandings, or a quiet de facto calm. The Saudi-Houthi border calm of 2022–2026 was de facto.
  - Agreements that only promise future talks.
  - Partial deals covering certain targets or places, e.g. "no strikes on ports" or a Hodeidah-only deal like Stockholm in 2018.
  - Statements that only "welcome" or "support" an agreement without accepting it.
- **Who decides:** Official Saudi government statements and Houthi leadership statements. A consensus of credible reporting can also be used.
- **Edge cases:**
  - **UN- or Oman-announced truces.** In April 2022 the UN announced the truce and the parties welcomed it. The deal would probably count only if both Riyadh and Houthi leaders (Abdul-Malik al-Houthi, the Supreme Political Council, or spokesman Mohammed Abdulsalam) clearly confirm they accept it.
  - **A short humanitarian pause.** Riyadh reportedly floated a two-week truce in September 2026. A pause like that would count if it is general and both sides accept a start date. Its length does not matter.
  - **A regional US–Iran deal.** Iran is demanding a "complete regional ceasefire" (Al Jazeera, Nov 2, 2026). A deal like that would only count if Saudi Arabia and the Houthis each announce acceptance of the same agreement. Iran or the US announcing it is not enough.
  - **Ambiguous announcements.** These trigger a review window of up to 7 days. An announcement in late December could therefore be ruled on after Dec 31, but the announcement itself must happen by Dec 31.
  - **Parties.** "Saudi Arabia" probably includes the Saudi-led coalition or Joint Forces Command. A deal only between the Yemeni government and the Houthis, without Saudi acceptance, is unclear.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates

**A. Saudi–Houthi agreed ceasefires during 2015–2022 (my own count from the historical record)**

| Date | Agreement | Would it qualify? |
|---|---|---|
| May 12, 2015 | 5-day humanitarian pause, proposed by Saudi Arabia, accepted by Houthis | Likely yes |
| Jul 2015 | UN truce; the coalition never clearly committed and it collapsed | Probably no |
| Dec 15, 2015 | Ceasefire tied to the Geneva talks | Likely yes |
| Apr 10, 2016 | Cessation of hostilities for the Kuwait talks | Likely yes |
| Apr 2, 2022 | UN-brokered nationwide truce | Likely yes |
| Apr 2020; Mar 2021 | Unilateral or one-sided Saudi offers | No |
| Dec 2018 | Stockholm agreement (Hodeidah only) | No |

- That gives about 4 qualifying agreements over roughly 90 months of open conflict (March 2015 to early 2022), or about 0.045 a month.
- Over the roughly 1.3 months left in this question, that implies about 6%.
- Agreements came in clusters. Three came within 13 months of the 2015 intervention, then none for six years.
- **Speed:** The first agreed pause in 2015 came about 7 weeks after Operation Decisive Storm began. The current Houthi attacks began around July 13, 2026 (about 4 months ago) and intensified in September. No agreement has appeared, even though Saudi Arabia reportedly offered a truce in September.

**B. Ceasefires brokered by Oman, Qatar or the US during the 2025–26 regional war**
- In that war, both sides of a dyad announcing acceptance of a deal has been fairly common:
  - The US and the Houthis reached an Oman-brokered deal in May 2025, about 7 weeks into a US bombing campaign (Al Jazeera, May 6, 2025).
  - Israel and Iran reached a ceasefire in June 2025.
  - The US and Iran signed a memorandum on June 17, 2026 setting a 60-day ceasefire "on all fronts" (Al Jazeera/PBS reporting).
- These deals also break down or lapse. Trump said in July that the June memorandum was "over."
- Within any given 6-week window, I estimate roughly 15–30% of high-intensity dyads in this regional war reached an announced ceasefire. This is a rough judgement, not a formal count.

**C. Hard-to-settle cases: the attacker demands structural concessions first**
- The Houthis demand that Saudi Arabia lift its air and sea blockade before any ceasefire (The National, Oct 27, 2026).
- In 2020–2021, Houthi demands of this kind led them to reject Saudi ceasefire offers for about two years, until the April 2022 truce. That truce included Sanaa flights and fuel ships, a partial version of the same demand.
- Base rate for this class over 6 weeks: low, probably under 10%.

## 3. Current state of the world (most recent first)

- **Nov 20–21, 2026: active escalation.**
  - The Saudi-led coalition hit Hodeidah port and targets in Sanaa, Saada and Taiz in a "special operation" against Houthi weapons-smuggling and their missile and drone capabilities.
  - The Houthis threatened Jeddah and other Red Sea ports, warned airlines that Saudi airspace was unsafe, and threatened the Shuqaiq power plant.
  - The coalition intercepted two ballistic missiles fired toward Jazan.
  - The UN warned that the Hodeidah strikes endanger humanitarian supply routes.
  - Source reports: Al Jazeera, Nov 20; The National, Nov 20; Washington Times, Nov 20; Gulf News; Alhurra.
- **Nov 19:** A Houthi strike damaged Riyadh's tallest tower, which houses the headquarters of the Public Investment Fund (PIF, Saudi Arabia's sovereign wealth fund). It was the second landmark in Riyadh's financial district damaged in under two weeks. Saudi Arabia says debris from an interception caused it; the Houthis say a direct hit (NBC; Al Jazeera, Nov 19).
- **Since Nov 7:** The Houthis have reportedly attacked Saudi Arabia every day, hitting military sites, airports and energy facilities. Saudi Arabia launched a major air campaign earlier the week of Nov 16 (Long War Journal, Nov 2026).
- **Early November:** Saudi-backed government forces withdrew near the Bab al-Mandab Strait, letting the Houthis expand their ground control (search summaries of Nov 20–21 reporting).
- **Nov 15:** The coalition intercepted 2 missiles and 2 drones aimed at Riyadh (Al Jazeera, Nov 15).
- **Nov 12:** The UN Security Council extended sanctions and condemned Houthi cross-border attacks on Saudi Arabia (Al Jazeera, Nov 12).
- **US–Iran track (Nov 2–10):**
  - Talks mediated by Qatar and Pakistan ended without a breakthrough, but contacts continued.
  - Iran wants a "complete regional ceasefire" as part of any deal.
  - On Nov 9, Trump said the war was "effectively over militarily."
  - There was talk of more strikes after the Nov 3 US midterms (Al Jazeera, Nov 1, 2 and 10; Al-Monitor, Nov 2026).
  - PBS reported a tentative US–Iran deal to extend the ceasefire and start nuclear talks, but I could not confirm its date.
- **Oct 30:** The Houthis claimed six attacks on Saudi airports and oil facilities right after the Tehran meeting (Al Jazeera, Oct 30).
- **Oct 26:** Saudi Defence Minister Khalid bin Salman, Pakistan's army chief Asim Munir and Turkey's intelligence chief Ibrahim Kalin met Iranian officials in Tehran.
  - They asked Iran to rein in the Houthis.
  - Iran said it lacks the leverage to do so.
  - The Houthis' condition that the blockade be lifted first is unchanged.
  - Saudi Arabia's foreign ministry stressed "opening channels of negotiation" (The National, Oct 27; Al-Monitor, Oct 2026).
- **Oct 12:** Iranian Foreign Minister Araghchi offered to help start Saudi–Houthi dialogue (Al Jazeera, Oct 12).
- **Oct 10:** A missile hit King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, killing about 12 people, the deadliest attack on the kingdom in more than a decade. Al-Houthi warned of a long war (CNN, Oct 8; Al Jazeera, Oct 11).
- **Saudi casualty toll:** Saudi authorities say at least 26 people have been killed in Houthi attacks since Sep 23.
- **Sep 22:** Trump reportedly froze US strikes on the Houthis that the Saudi crown prince had requested.
  - Riyadh then reportedly offered, through Oman, a 2-week ceasefire plus direct talks.
  - The source is Lebanon's Al-Akhbar newspaper; Riyadh has not confirmed it.
  - The Houthis gave no final answer and insist on a comprehensive settlement: end of the war, lifting of the blockade, and their humanitarian demands.
  - Saudi official Rayed Krimly said the Houthis "rejected a meeting offer in Oman."
  - Source reports: Israel Hayom, Sep 22; Jerusalem Post; TBS News.
  - US officials also met Houthi representatives in Oman. The Houthis pledged not to attack US ships but said Saudi ships and oil cargoes remain targets.
- **September:** A major Houthi ground offensive on the Red Sea coast. On Sep 10, UN envoy Hans Grundberg called this a "new, more dangerous phase." A prisoner swap planned for July stalled (House of Commons Library, Oct 5).
- **Jul 13–20:** The war restarted.
  - The trigger was an incident involving a Houthi delegation's plane returning from the funeral of Iran's Supreme Leader (Arab Center DC).
  - The Houthis declared the truce with Saudi Arabia and the Yemeni government over.
  - Al-Houthi announced a "blockade for blockade, airport for airport" doctrine and a naval blockade on Saudi Arabia (around Jul 20).
- **Background:**
  - The de facto Saudi–Houthi calm had held since the April 2022 truce.
  - UN Military Coordination Committee meetings between Saudi and Houthi representatives took place in Amman in April–June 2026.
  - Saudi-backed forces dismantled the separatist Southern Transitional Council in January 2026, opening a rift with the UAE.
  - The Strait of Hormuz closure pushed Saudi oil exports through the Red Sea, raising the stakes for Riyadh (Al Jazeera analysis).

## 4. Drivers and scenarios

**Paths to YES**
1. **An Oman-brokered truce, which I judge the most plausible path.**
   - Saudi Arabia has already shown it wants a pause: the September 2-week offer and the push for negotiation channels.
   - Rising costs push Riyadh toward a deal: hits on Riyadh's financial district, Red Sea export routes under threat, airport attacks.
   - YES would need Saudi Arabia to offer something on the blockade, such as Sanaa airport flights or Hodeidah port access, as in 2022.
   - The Houthis would also need to see their gains on the ground as enough.
2. **A US–Iran comprehensive deal that includes a regional ceasefire, with Iran pressing the Houthis.** This counts only if Saudi Arabia and the Houthis each announce acceptance. Iran says it lacks leverage, which may be posturing.
3. **US pressure on both sides:** Trump wants low oil prices and has avoided a new front. That could push Saudi Arabia to concede and lead Oman or the US to announce a deal.
4. **A UN-announced truce or humanitarian pause** that both sides formally accept, for example around the holidays or to protect humanitarian access to Hodeidah.

**Paths to NO**
1. Escalation continues. The fighting has been growing through Nov 21: daily Houthi strikes, a new Saudi air campaign, threats against Hodeidah and Jeddah.
2. The Houthis keep demanding the blockade be lifted first, and Saudi Arabia refuses to concede under fire.
3. Fighting dies down only informally, through a de facto lull or unilateral restraint. That would not count, and it is how 2022–2026 actually worked.
4. A narrow deal, such as a ports-for-ports or airports-for-airports understanding. This fits al-Houthi's reciprocity doctrine and would not count.
5. An announced deal where one side only "welcomes" it, or the two sides describe it differently. That creates ambiguity that could resolve NO.
6. Too little time: only about 40 days remain, including the end-of-year holiday period.

## 5. Signposts

**Would raise the probability (strongly):**
- Oman's foreign ministry confirming that Saudi and Houthi delegations are meeting in Muscat.
- A Saudi delegation visiting Sanaa, as in April 2023.
- Mohammed Abdulsalam announcing a "positive response" to a proposal.

**Would raise the probability (moderately):**
- Saudi Arabia easing the blockade: Sanaa flights, Hodeidah fuel ships.
- The Houthis pausing attacks for several days.
- A US–Iran deal text that names Yemen.
- Grundberg announcing a framework.

**Would lower the probability:**
- Coalition strikes on Hodeidah port infrastructure continuing.
- Houthi strikes on Jeddah or Shuqaiq.
- Mass-casualty attacks in Saudi Arabia.
- A US return to striking the Houthis.
- US–Iran talks collapsing.
- Renewed US or Israeli strikes on Iran after the midterms.
- Saudi rhetoric that diplomacy has failed (Responsible Statecraft's reading of Riyadh's view).

**Would narrow how it resolves:** Any deal limited to particular target types, such as energy or airports, would resolve NO even if fighting drops.

## 6. Uncertainties

- I could not see anything from Nov 10 to Nov 21 on any Saudi–Houthi mediation. Quiet backchannel talks may exist without being reported.
- I could not confirm whether the Houthis formally rejected the September 2-week proposal; the only claim is from Saudi official Rayed Krimly.
- How the June 17, 2026 US–Iran memorandum's "all fronts" ceasefire treated Yemen, and whether the Houthis and Saudi Arabia accepted it, is unclear. This bears on whether a new US–Iran deal could carry a qualifying Saudi–Houthi ceasefire with it.
- The status and date of the reported tentative US–Iran deal to extend the ceasefire are unclear.
- How much influence Iran really has over the Houthis is disputed.
- The exact trigger of the July collapse, and Saudi Arabia's current formal negotiating position, are not established.
- Some casualty and strike figures conflict between Saudi and Houthi sources.
