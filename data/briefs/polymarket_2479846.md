# Research brief: Will Google and SpaceX officially announce an orbital data center or orbital computing agreement by Dec 31, 2026?

## 1. Resolution mechanics

- **What counts as YES:** Google LLC, Alphabet or SpaceX (or their authorized representatives) must officially announce an agreement between Google and SpaceX that relates to orbital data centers or orbital computing infrastructure. Qualifying types are launch agreements, strategic partnerships, joint ventures, infrastructure agreements or other formal commercial arrangements. The deal does not need to have closed or been fully executed.
- **Deadline:** Dec 31, 2026, 11:59 PM ET. About 6 weeks remain from today (Nov 18, 2026).
- **Sources:** Official statements from Google, Alphabet or SpaceX come first. A "consensus of credible reporting" can also be used.
- **What does not count:** reports of negotiations or talks, rumors, non-binding discussions, and agreements unrelated to orbital compute. The confirmed ground-based compute deal (Google pays SpaceX/xAI about $920M a month for roughly 110,000 Nvidia GPUs, Oct 2026 to Jun 2029) is not orbital, so it does not qualify on its own (CNBC, Jun 5, 2026; TechCrunch, Jun 5, 2026).

**Key ambiguity: the Oct 1, 2026 launch.** Google's first Project Suncatcher prototype satellite reached orbit on Oct 1, 2026 aboard SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare (a Falcon 9). Planet built the satellite, which carries four Trillium TPUs. Google posted: "in partnership with @planet, we launched a prototype satellite carrying four TPUs into orbit on @SpaceX's Transporter-18 rideshare mission" (Google on X, Oct 1, 2026; Google blog, Oct 2026; CNBC, Oct 1, 2026).

- **Case for YES:** SpaceX physically launched Google's orbital compute hardware, and some outlets call SpaceX Google's "launch partner." Teslarati's headline was "Google just picked SpaceX for its first step into orbital AI."
- **Case against:** Neither company announced a Google–SpaceX agreement. The ride appears to have been arranged through Planet; one report notes it rode "on a Planet Labs satellite," and Planet has its own rideshare relationship with SpaceX. A launch that has already happened is not the same as an announced agreement.
- **What the open market suggests:** The question is still being asked on Nov 18, after the launch. That suggests the resolver has not treated the Oct 1 launch as qualifying, or that the market was created after it. Forecasters should check the market's creation date and any clarifications or comment-thread rulings, because this edge case could decide the outcome.
- **Other edge cases:**
  - A launch contract for Google's 2027 two-satellite mission that is announced by Planet, not by Google or SpaceX. It probably qualifies only if Google or SpaceX confirms it, or through "consensus of credible reporting."
  - SpaceX selling orbital compute ("Starmind") capacity to Google.
  - A SpaceX 10-Q or 8-K disclosure of a contract.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates

1. **Reported big-tech talks that become an announced deal within a fixed window.** The WSJ reported the Google–SpaceX launch talks on May 12, 2026, and Google confirmed it had held "discussions with SpaceX and others regarding future launches" (Epoch Times/Reuters, May 12, 2026; Forbes, May 12, 2026). Rough heuristic: about 40–60% of reported "in talks" deals happen at some point, spread over 1–2+ years. This is my own rough estimate, not a measured statistic.
   - Six months have passed with no announcement. A constant-hazard model gives roughly 3–8% for any 6-week window from here.
   - The parallel ground compute deal went from talks to disclosure fast (SpaceX's IPO filing, Jun 5, 2026). The orbital track has not.
2. **How small rideshare deals get announced.** Rideshare slots on SpaceX's Transporter and Bandwagon missions are almost never announced as standalone "agreements." They usually show up only around launch, often through the satellite builder (here, Planet). Google's 2027 two-satellite mission, built by Planet, would most likely follow that pattern (Space.com, Sep 2026; NYT via secondary sources). So most launches of this type never produce a formal Google–SpaceX announcement.
3. **Late-December corporate announcements.** Big partnership announcements cluster around earnings, conferences and launch events. The Dec 15–31 holiday period is usually quiet. No Google or SpaceX event in the window is known to be a natural place for an orbital-compute announcement (no Suncatcher launch is scheduled before the end of 2026).

## 3. Current state of the world (most recent first)

- **Oct 28, 2026:** On Alphabet's Q3 call, Pichai listed launching TPUs into space through Project Suncatcher as a highlight. Nothing found mentions SpaceX or a launch contract (Alphabet Q3 2026 transcript / Google blog, Oct 28, 2026).
- **Oct 30, 2026 (single source, unconfirmed):** The satellite reportedly ran a Gemma 4 model in orbit.
- **Oct 1, 2026:** The Suncatcher prototype launched on Transporter-18, one of about 130 payloads. Google published a peer-reviewed version of its white paper in *Joule*.
  - Google's analysis says Starship would need about 1,800 cumulative launches to bring launch costs to about $200/kg by around 2035. That framing points to a long timeline before scaled orbital data centers (TechCrunch, Oct 1, 2026; CNN, Oct 1, 2026).
  - Fortune (Oct 10, 2026) surveyed the competitive race, including SpaceX and Blue Origin, which has its own plan for a constellation of roughly 52,000 satellites.
- **Next step:** Two Planet-built satellites in 2027 to test laser links between spacecraft. No launch provider has been publicly named (secondary reports, Oct 2026).
- **SpaceX's own orbital compute:** SpaceX has filed with regulators for up to 1 million data-center satellites and targets its first "Starmind" AI satellites (Nvidia chips) for Q4 2027. Its IPO prospectus warned that orbital data centers "may not achieve commercial viability" (The Next Web; secondary sources, 2026).
- **Jun 12, 2026:** SpaceX went public on Nasdaq (SPCX), raising about $75B at about $1.77T. As a public company, it must disclose material contracts in its filings (CBS News, Jun 12, 2026).
- **Jun 5, 2026:** SpaceX disclosed the Google Cloud Service Agreement for ground-based GPU capacity at xAI data centers. Either side can terminate with 90 days' notice after Dec 31, 2026. Google holds roughly 5–6% of SpaceX (CNBC; TechCrunch; Implicator, Jun 2026).
- **May 12–13, 2026:** The WSJ reported Google negotiating a launch deal with SpaceX for orbital data-center units and also talking to other launch providers. Google confirmed only "discussions" (GV Wire/WSJ; Forbes; Epoch Times, May 12, 2026).
- **Background:**
  - Suncatcher was announced Nov 4, 2025, with plans for 81-satellite clusters.
  - SpaceX merged with xAI in February 2026.
  - Anthropic's SpaceX compute deal reportedly mentioned possible future orbital collaboration, so SpaceX does bundle orbital language into compute deals.

**Scheduled events before Dec 31:** No Suncatcher launch, Alphabet earnings or SpaceX earnings call fall in the window. SpaceX Transporter and Bandwagon rideshares continue on their usual cadence, but the 2027 Google satellites are not expected to fly in 2026.

## 4. Drivers and scenarios

**Paths to YES:**
- **(a)** Google or SpaceX announces a launch contract for the 2027 Suncatcher pair, or a multi-launch, Starship-scale agreement. This depends on the talks maturing and on someone choosing to publicize it. SpaceX has an incentive to show off orbital-AI customers after its IPO.
- **(b)** A strategic orbital-compute partnership: Google TPUs on SpaceX/Starmind satellites, or an extension of the $920M/month deal to cover orbital capacity. This would most likely appear in a SpaceX SEC filing or a joint press release.
- **(c)** The resolver decides retroactively, or after a dispute, that the Oct 1 Transporter-18 launch, or Google's public statements about it, counts as an announced launch arrangement.
- **(d)** Credible outlets (WSJ, Bloomberg, Reuters) report a signed deal, and that reporting is accepted as consensus.

**Paths to NO:**
- The talks stay talks.
- The 2027 launch is booked through Planet's existing SpaceX rideshare contract and never announced as a Google–SpaceX agreement.
- Any announcement slips into 2027, for example timed to the next Suncatcher launch, Alphabet's Q4 earnings in early February, or a SpaceX event.
- Google spreads its launches across other providers.

Google's own *Joule* paper puts economic viability around 2035, which lowers the urgency for a big commercial deal now.

## 5. Signposts

These would raise the probability sharply:
- A SpaceX 8-K or press release naming Google as an orbital-compute or launch customer.
- A Google Research or Planet blog naming SpaceX as the 2027 launch provider.
- Musk posting about a Google orbital deal.
- WSJ or Bloomberg reporting a signed deal.
- The market resolver clarifying that the Oct 1 launch counts.

These would push the probability down:
- A clarification that rideshare bookings made through third parties do not count.
- Google naming a different launch provider.
- No news by mid-December.
- Either side signaling termination of the ground compute deal after Dec 31, which would be a sign of a weaker relationship.
