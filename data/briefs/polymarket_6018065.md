# Research brief: Will the Houthis capture Taiz city by December 31, 2026?

*Prepared November 22, 2026. My page-fetch tool was blocked for every site I tried, so this brief rests on search-result summaries of the cited articles and not on full-text reads. Wikipedia timeline items are treated as provisional.*

## 1. Resolution mechanics

- **What counts as YES:** By 11:59 PM ET on December 31, 2026, the Houthis (Ansar Allah, or forces acting under its direction) must have established **primary military control over Al-Qahira (Cairo) Castle** in Taiz city. Controlling an area that includes the castle is enough, unless credible reporting says the castle itself is still outside Houthi control.
- **What does not count:** Siege or encirclement of the city (which has been in place since early October 2026), shelling, raids, infiltration, isolated incursions, temporary disruption, or claims and announcements without real control. Control that is "credibly contested or disputed" also fails.
- **Negotiated outcomes:** A handover through a deal, surrender or ceasefire counts only once Houthi control is actually in place.
- **Timing edge case:** If it is unclear on December 31 whether a capture happened, the market can stay open for 3 extra days, to January 3, 2027. Control must still have been established by December 31. If it is not confirmed by the end of January 3, the market resolves NO.
- **Who decides:** A consensus of credible reporting plus photo or video evidence.
- **Where the castle is:** Al-Qahira sits on the northern slope of Jabal Sabr, above the old city center (Al-Qahira Castle, Wikipedia). It is deep inside the government-held core of the city. A Houthi foothold on the outer parts of Jabal Sabr does not equal control of the castle.
- **Ambiguities:**
  - Fighting inside the city would almost certainly be "contested" for some time before control counts as clear.
  - Government sources would dispute any Houthi claim.
  - Fast collapses usually produce Houthi video quickly. Mocha in September 2026 had Reuters confirmation from 4 government military sources within hours.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates

1. **Houthi attempts on large, defended cities since 2014.**
   - Sanaa (2014): fell in days, but through army defection and a political deal.
   - Aden (2015): Houthis partly entered, then were expelled within about 4 months.
   - Taiz (2015–2026): never fully taken in about 11 years. Local fighters "armed largely with personal weapons" stopped full control in 2015 (The National, Oct 3–4, 2026). The Houthis did briefly hold Al-Qahira in 2015 before the resistance took it back (my own background knowledge, roughly August 2015; not re-checked).
   - Marib (2020–2022): failed despite a sustained offensive that reached the city's approaches.
   - Mocha (Sep 10, 2026): fell "after hours" amid government disarray (France 24 / Al Jazeera, Sep 10, 2026). One analyst blamed "a discombobulation of the anti-Houthi forces."
   - Tally: about 1–2 quick captures out of about 5 major attempts. The quick ones happened when defenders collapsed politically, not when they were dug in. Dug-in defenders with a local identity, as in Taiz and Marib, held 2 of 2 times.
2. **Fully encircled cities with entrenched local defenders (recent wars).** Time from full encirclement to fall:
   - East Aleppo: about 5 months (July to December 2016).
   - Mariupol: about 3 months.
   - El Fasher: about 18 months (fell October 2025).
   - Taiz 2015–16: the siege was partly broken rather than ending in a fall.
   - Rough base rate for a fall within about 3 months of encirclement (Taiz was encircled about October 4, so December 31 is about 12–13 weeks in): perhaps 10–20%. It is lower when the defenders get air support and have mountainous terrain.
3. **Houthi momentum in 2026.** The Houthis have taken nearly every objective they pursued on the Red Sea coast: Mocha, Dhubab, Bab al-Mandeb, Perim Island, and the Al-Maqatirah and Al-Madaribah districts of Lahj (FDD, Sep 14; Long War Journal, Nov 2026). On the Taiz city fronts, by contrast, government sources have reported repelling assaults repeatedly for 3 months. Al Jazeera described a "stalemate" and said the mountain terrain had halted Houthi advances (Al Jazeera, Oct 1 and Oct 4, 2026).

## 3. Current state of the world (most recent first)

**November 21–22**
- Government forces say they repelled Houthi attacks at Jabal Han and the old Taiz airport, near the city's western entrance. They also claim to have killed a senior Houthi commander.
- Coalition warplanes struck Houthi sites in 6 districts of Taiz and Lahj, including missile launchpads at the 22nd Armored Brigade camp in Al-Janad, east of Taiz (Yemen Monitor, ~Nov 21–22).
- The Houthis struck Riyadh in reprisal for a coalition "special operation" in Hodeidah, Sanaa, Saada and Taiz (Dawn / Tribune, Nov 21, 2026).
- The government's Siege Affairs Office reported 1 killed and 7 wounded in the city on Nov 21 (Middle East Eye, Nov 21).

**November 18–20**
- Al Jazeera's reporter in Taiz described "fierce battles" and said villages on Jabal Sabr are "under a complete siege." The coalition hit 60 Houthi military sites (Al Jazeera, Nov 18, 2026).
- The government says it repelled attacks on the Al-Aqroud and Kahlan fronts on Nov 19.
- The coalition carried out strikes near Taiz and at a power station in Mocha (CFR tracker, Nov 18–20).

**November 15–17**
- The Houthis advanced to about 60 km (about 40 miles) from Aden. They took Al-Maqatirah, which carries the main Aden–Taiz road, and Al-Madaribah.
- Southern forces withdrew from Dhubab airport and the Bab al-Mandeb area.
- The government is redeploying troops to defend Aden, with Aden given priority (France 24, Nov 15; Japan Times / AFP, Nov 16; Long War Journal, Nov 2026).
- Saudi Arabia replaced its Joint Forces commander with Gen. Fayyad al-Ruwaili after the setbacks (CGTN, Nov 17).
- Al-Monitor reports that Riyadh expects the war to run into 2027 (up to about 6 more months) and wants ground gains before any talks.
- The Houthis released 600 detained government fighters, calling it a humanitarian gesture.

**November 13**
- The government says it repelled a "large-scale" Houthi attack on the Al-Aqroud front, southeast of the city (MEE, Nov 13).
- The Siege Affairs Office counted 44 dead and 37 injured over 10 days (mid-November).

**Early November**
- Each side blamed the other for a strike on al-Hadara market in al-Waziiya district that killed about 30 people (Al Jazeera, Nov 6).
- Government forces claimed advances elsewhere, including al-Jawf and Marib (Al Jazeera, Nov 7 and 9).

**October**
- Late October: the Houthis advanced onto Jabal Sabr, the mountain directly overlooking the city, and fighting spread to the Al-Aqroudh and Al-Masrakh fronts (Al Jazeera, Oct 29).
- Human Rights Watch reported a "full encirclement" of the city, food and medicine shortages, the Hayjat al-Abd road in al-Turbah blown up on Oct 18, and an Oct 1 culvert attack at al-Muharraqah (HRW, Oct 26 and 29).
- The UN reported about 260,000 people displaced, about half of them sheltering in Taiz (Al Jazeera, Oct 22).
- Taiz governor Nabil Shamsan says the Houthis have massed about 60,000 fighters and the city is near famine (Sabq).
- Alhurra reported that government forces were "losing ground around Taiz."
- Early October (by about Oct 4): the Houthis took al-Turbah and parts of al-Misrakh, cutting the Taiz–Turbah–Aden road and encircling the city (Al Jazeera, Oct 4 and 10; Al-Monitor explainer). The Houthis hold areas north, east and west of the city. Government forces hold the city and its immediate surroundings.

**September**
- The Houthis took Mocha on Sep 10, then the rest of the Red Sea coast and Perim Island (FDD, Sep 14).
- A western Taiz offensive starting around Sep 3 was described as the fiercest ground fighting since 2022.

**Wider context**
- 2026 saw the Southern Transitional Council collapse and the Houthi–Saudi war restart (House of Commons Library briefing).
- I found no active peace or ceasefire negotiations as of late November.

**Scheduled events before December 31:** I found none, no talks rounds or deadlines. Weather and seasonal conditions are not a major constraint.

## 4. Drivers and scenarios

**Paths to YES (by December 31):**
- **Collapse from starvation or attrition.** The siege degrades supplies and morale and the defense breaks. This depends on how long food and ammunition last, and whether the coalition can airdrop or reopen a corridor.
- **Jabal Sabr breakthrough.** Taking the heights south of the city would put the old city and the castle under direct fire, which could force a withdrawal from central districts.
- **Political or military abandonment.** The government and Saudis prioritize Aden, pull units or stop paying Taiz forces, and local commanders cut deals or surrender, as in the Mocha-style disarray. This depends on the cohesion of the Islah-aligned Taiz Military Axis and on Saudi decisions.
- **Negotiated handover.** This would only count once Houthi control is actually established. It looks very unlikely by December 31 given that no talks are under way.

**Paths to NO:**
- **The status quo siege continues.** This is the 11-year default. Dense urban terrain, mountains, highly motivated local resistance, and growing Saudi air support (60 sites struck on Nov 18, new commander) all favor the defense.
- **Houthi effort goes elsewhere.** The Houthi main effort may stay focused on Aden and Lahj, leaving Taiz besieged but not stormed.
- **Partial gains only.** Even if the Houthis gain ground in parts of the city, a contested status fails the resolution rules.

## 5. Signposts

**Toward YES (strongly):**
- Confirmed Houthi control of the Jabal Sabr summit or Al-Aqroud / Al-Masrakh, giving them the southern approach.
- Fighting reported in central districts such as Bab Musa, the old city, or Al-Jumhuri hospital area.
- Reports of commanders defecting or units withdrawing.
- The government or Saudis openly redeploying Taiz units to Aden.
- Famine declarations or ammunition shortages.
- Reuters or AFP citing government sources that lines have collapsed.

**Toward NO:**
- Government forces reopening the al-Turbah / Hayjat al-Abd road or the western corridor.
- Continued routine "repelled" reports with no change in lines.
- Houthi forces committed to an assault on Aden.
- A ceasefire or truce, especially one that freezes the lines.
- Successful air resupply or airdrops.

**Ambiguous:** Houthi media claims of "liberating" districts without independent video. Under the rules these do not qualify.

## 6. Uncertainties

- **Exact front lines inside and around the city.** I could not establish how close Houthi forces are to Al-Qahira or whether Jabal Sabr is held or contested. Reporting says "advanced onto" Jabal Sabr and that Sabr villages are besieged.
- **Defenders' supplies and morale.** Food, fuel and ammunition stocks are unclear, and so is whether any supply route (for example, mountain tracks through Sabr or al-Misrakh) is still open.
- **Real troop numbers.** The governor's figure of 60,000 Houthi fighters cannot be verified.
- **Partisan sourcing.** Almost all front-line reporting comes from government claims or Houthi media.
- **Saudi strategy.** It is unclear whether Riyadh will reinforce Taiz or concentrate on Aden.
- **Market creation date.** It is unknown, but irrelevant unless a capture had already happened, and none has been reported.
- **Wikipedia items.** Several, such as the "Fall of Aden (2026)" page title, were seen only as search snippets. Their exact content is unconfirmed; that page probably refers to the earlier 2026 events involving the Southern Transitional Council.
