# Research brief: Will the Fed raise rates again between Sep 17 and Dec 9, 2026?

*Prepared 2026-11-12. On purpose, this brief leaves out prediction-market prices and fed-funds-futures probabilities for this event. Several of my sources quoted them, and I did not carry those numbers over. The Fed's website and most article pages refused my fetch attempts, so most facts below come from search-result summaries of dated news reports, not from the primary documents. Where sources disagreed, I say so.*

## 1. Resolution mechanics
- **What counts as YES:** the **upper bound** of the federal funds target range goes up at any point from **Sep 17, 2026** through the announcement of the **Dec 8–9, 2026** FOMC decision. Today the range is 3.75%–4.00%, so YES means an upper bound of 4.25% or higher. A 25bp or 50bp hike both count.
- **Excluded:** the Sep 16, 2026 hike (3.50–3.75% → 3.75–4.00%). The Oct 27–28 meeting already held, so in practice **YES depends almost entirely on the Dec 9 decision**. The only other route is an emergency hike between meetings. The last one was in April 1994, so that is very unlikely.
- **Timing:** the decision comes out around 2:00 pm ET on Dec 9. The market closes Dec 10 at 04:59 UTC, which is 11:59 pm ET on Dec 9. It cannot resolve NO before the December decision is published.
- **Edge cases:**
  - Changes only to IORB/ON RRP rates, or other technical adjustments, do not count. Only the target range counts.
  - If the meeting were moved to after Dec 9, the rules are unclear. Meeting dates are tentative until confirmed at the previous meeting. Warsh's plan to cut back on rate-setting meetings starts in **2027**, and the Dec 8–9 date is cited consistently (Fed to cut back on rate-setting meetings, Jul 31, 2026; WRAPUP/Union Leader, Nov 2026).
  - Resolution source: federalreserve.gov, with "consensus of credible reporting" as a backup.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates
**A. A September dot plot calls for more tightening by year-end. Does the Fed deliver by December?** (Counted from my own knowledge of SEP history.)
- 2015: Sep median showed liftoff in 2015 → delivered Dec 2015.
- 2016: Sep median showed one hike → delivered Dec 2016.
- 2017: one more → delivered Dec 2017.
- 2018: one more → delivered Dec 2018.
- 2022: more hikes → delivered.
- 2023: Sep median showed one more 2023 hike → **not delivered**. Inflation cooled, the 10-year hit about 5%, and the Fed said tighter financial conditions were "doing the work."
- **Result: about 5 of 6 (~83%).** The 2023 miss is the closest parallel to now: the 10-year is at 5.00% (Nov 10, 2026), and Warsh has already pointed to tighter market conditions doing the Fed's work.

**B. A strongly signalled next-meeting hike gets derailed in the final ~4 weeks.**
- Examples of derailment: Sep 2015 (China market turmoil), Jun 2016 (weak May payrolls of +38k, released 5 days before the meeting), and Nov/Dec 2023.
- Since the mid-1990s, when the committee, the consensus and the data all point the same way about a month out, the Fed has almost always followed through. My rough estimate is that over 85–90% of such cases end in the move. This is an estimate, not a precise count.

**C. Previous-meeting dissents in favor of a move.** Two voters dissented for a hike in October. Hawkish dissents usually come before the committee moves in that direction within one to two meetings (for example, 2015–16 and 2018). This is a qualitative pattern, not an exact rate.

## 4. Drivers and scenarios
**Paths to YES**
- **Base case:** a 25bp hike on Dec 9. This follows from the Sep dots, two hawkish dissents in October, a hot October CPI and strong jobs. It needs October PCE and the November jobs report to avoid a sharp miss to the downside.
- **Hawkish variant:** a 50bp hike, pushed by Waller and Logan. It still resolves YES.
- **Emergency intermeeting hike:** negligible chance.

**Paths to NO**
1. **Financial-conditions argument:** the 10-year at 5%+ plus an equity selloff lets Warsh argue that markets are tightening for the Fed, as in 2023. He has used this argument before.
2. **Downside data surprise:** a weak November payrolls number on Dec 4 (like June 2016), or soft PCE.
3. **Market or geopolitical shock:** a credit event or a sharp selloff.
4. **Chair-led hold:** Warsh, who has not submitted a dot, steers toward patience against a hawkish committee. Unlikely, given that the September vote was unanimous and the October dissents were hawkish.
5. **Political or institutional disruption:** for example, a change in board membership. Low probability before December.

## 5. Signposts
- **Toward YES:**
  - Warsh speeches before the blackout that endorse further tightening.
  - October core PCE at 0.3% or higher.
  - November payrolls above 100k with unemployment at or below 4.1%.
  - Doves (Jefferson, Williams) openly backing a December move.
  - A Nikileaks-style "WSJ Fed whisperer" preview before the meeting.
- **Toward NO:**
  - Warsh or Williams stressing tighter financial conditions or a "wait and see" stance.
  - A November payrolls miss or a jump in unemployment.
  - Core PCE at 0.2% or lower.
  - A sharp market selloff or credit stress.
  - Pre-meeting press previews framing December as "live, but leaning hold."
- **Biggest single swing factors:** the tone of Warsh's own remarks, and the Dec 4 jobs report.

## 6. Uncertainties
- I could not read primary Fed documents (statements, SEP, transcripts) or the Reuters poll. All facts rest on secondary summaries, a few of which were garbled.
- Warsh's own view on December is unclear. He gives deliberately little forward guidance and skipped the dots.
- I found no post-CPI views from Jefferson, Williams or Kashkari. One pre-release piece quoted Kashkari as saying CPI was "heading in the right direction," which may be from an earlier month.
- The exact release dates for October PCE and November CPI are unconfirmed.
- Whether the Cook litigation or other political moves could change the board's makeup before Dec 9.
