# Research brief: Will HHS/CDC adopt a federal childhood schedule that routinely recommends vaccines against fewer than 11 diseases before Jan 1, 2027?

## 1. Resolution mechanics

- **What counts as YES:** HHS or CDC must *announce* a federal childhood and adolescent immunization schedule that is *officially adopted* and *routinely* recommends vaccines against **fewer than 11 diseases**. This must happen before Jan 1, 2027 (close is 04:59 UTC on Jan 1, about 6 weeks from today, Nov 21, 2026).
- **The threshold sits just below the administration's own cut.** The January 5, 2026 schedule from the CDC acting director routinely recommended vaccines against exactly 11 diseases, down from about 17 (WTOP, Jul 2026; HHS fact sheet, Jan 2026). HHS described the 11 as "10 diseases of international consensus plus varicella." Those are measles, mumps, rubella, polio, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, Hib, pneumococcal and HPV, plus varicella. Flu, COVID, rotavirus, hepatitis A, hepatitis B, meningococcal and RSV moved to "high-risk" or "shared clinical decision-making" tiers. Re-adopting or reinstating that schedule would therefore **not** be enough for YES. A YES needs a further cut, most plausibly dropping varicella, which Denmark-style schedules don't routinely include, or another core antigen.
- **Edge cases and ambiguities:**
  - (a) How diseases are counted. Combination vaccines and HPV's cancer framing could be counted differently. Whether a "shared clinical decision-making" tier counts as "routine" is undefined; under HHS's own framing it does not.
  - (b) Whether an announced schedule that a court has stayed, or one with a future effective date, counts as "officially adopted." Strict wording points to adoption by the CDC director, not just a proposal or an ACIP vote.
  - (c) A schedule announced in late December but published in MMWR in January would probably count if the announcement says it is adopted.
  - (d) Who decides is not stated. Presumably the market creator, based on official HHS/CDC announcements.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates

1. **Direction of change to the CDC childhood schedule, 1983–2025.** The schedule grew from about 7 diseases (1983) to about 16–18 (2025), mostly through adding antigens. It was never cut by disease count until January 2026. That gives 1 reduction in roughly 40+ annual schedules (about 2–3%), and that single reduction happened under the current leadership. Base rate for any reduction in a given year: very low historically, but high under current leadership absent courts.
2. **Speed of executive vaccine-schedule action under this administration.** The presidential memorandum of December 5, 2025 led to the CDC adopting the 11-disease schedule on January 5, 2026, about 1 month later (CDC release, Jan 2026). So a one-month turnaround has happened once, but that was before any court constraint.
3. **Agency action while its predecessor action is stayed by a court.** Since the March 16, 2026 stay, there have been 0 schedule changes in about 8 months. ACIP meetings in Feb/March, June, and the one planned for later in October were postponed or canceled, and ACIP has not met since the ruling (The Hill, Oct 6 2026; CIDRAP, May 2026). Agencies under APA stays rarely re-issue the same or more aggressive action within weeks. They usually wait for the appeal.

The government appealed **only the stay of the 13 ACIP appointments, not the stay of the January 2026 schedule** (The Hill, Oct 6 2026; Yahoo/Hill). The judges probed the scope of the remedy and the plaintiffs' standing. No ruling came from the bench, and the ACIP meeting planned for later in October was expected to be canceled (yournews/AP, Oct 6 2026).

- **Jun 3, 2026:** Executive Order 14407, signed May 29, was published in the Federal Register. It directs CDC and ACIP to review HHS's peer-country assessment and update the childhood schedule "to the extent permitted by law," with "maximum flexibility" on timing and sequencing (White House, May 29 2026; Federal Register, Jun 3 2026). The June 24–26 ACIP meeting was later canceled because of the litigation.
- **May 19, 2026:** HHS re-chartered ACIP, replacing a withdrawn April 6 charter. The new charter moves governance to the CDC director and drops fixed meeting schedules. Kennedy can apparently appoint new members (Drug Topics; CIDRAP, May 28 2026).
- **Apr 29, 2026:** HHS appealed (STAT, Apr 29 2026).
- **Mar 16, 2026:** Judge Brian Murphy (D. Mass.) stayed four things: the January 5, 2026 schedule memo, the appointments of 13 ACIP members, all ACIP votes after June 11, 2025, and (in effect) the schedule changes. He held the CDC director likely acted contrary to law by changing the schedule without sufficiently consulting ACIP. The pre-2025 (June 2024 / July 2025 posted) schedule is back in effect (Georgetown CCF, Mar 31 2026; CRS R48982, Jun 11 2026).
- **Feb 27, 2026:** Two additional ACIP members were appointed, Downing and Farella (STAT). They were not among the stayed appointees, but ACIP still lacks a quorum.
- **Jan 5, 2026:** The 11-disease schedule was adopted by the CDC acting director without an ACIP vote.

**Scheduled events before close:** none confirmed. Possible but unscheduled: a First Circuit decision, new ACIP appointments, an ACIP meeting (normally announced in the Federal Register about 15 days ahead), and the annual schedule publication (usually Jan–Feb).

## 4. Drivers and scenarios

**Paths to YES (all need several steps inside about 6 weeks):**
- **A. Appeal win, then fast ACIP action.** The First Circuit vacates the appointments stay in late Nov or early Dec. ACIP quickly gets a meeting notice out, votes on a schedule that is more aggressive than the January one (for example, varicella dropped to align with Denmark), and the CDC director adopts it before Dec 31. This requires the appeal ruling, roughly 15 days' meeting notice, a vote, and adoption. It is very tight even in the best case. Note that the government did not appeal the schedule stay itself.
- **B. Rebuild ACIP without waiting for the court.** Kennedy appoints new members under the May 2026 charter, reaches a quorum, and votes under EO 14407. I found no reporting that this has started.
- **C. Unilateral adoption.** The CDC director or HHS adopts a schedule without ACIP. This would likely defy the reasoning of the March ruling and invite an immediate stay. Even if announced, it could still count as "announced as officially adopted," which is a resolution ambiguity.

**Paths to NO:**
- The status quo holds: ACIP has no quorum and there is no appellate ruling, or a ruling comes too late.
- Even if action resumes, the administration's flagship proposal was 11 diseases, not fewer. Re-adopting it does not resolve YES.
- The measles resurgence and loss of elimination status make further cuts politically costlier.

## 5. Signposts

- **Toward YES (strong):**
  - A First Circuit order vacating the appointments stay.
  - A Federal Register notice of a December ACIP meeting with a childhood-schedule agenda.
  - HHS announcing new ACIP members.
  - Any HHS or CDC statement proposing to move varicella (or another core disease) out of routine recommendations.
  - A CDC director memo modeled on the January 5 process.
- **Toward NO:**
  - No ACIP meeting notice by about Dec 10, which makes a valid ACIP-route adoption before Jan 1 nearly impossible.
  - A First Circuit decision upholding the stay.
  - CDC publishing a 2027 schedule that keeps the restored pre-2025 framework.
  - Rhetoric from HHS restricted to "restoring" the 11-disease schedule.
