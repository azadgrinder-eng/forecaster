# Research brief: Will the Fed's upper bound reach 4.25% or higher before 2027?

## 1. Resolution mechanics

- **What counts as YES:** Either bound of the FOMC's federal funds target range must be at or above 4.25%, as shown on the Fed's open-market page, at some point on or before December 31, 2026, 12:59 PM ET. The close date is listed as 2026-12-31 00:00Z, so a decision would have to come before then.
- **Current range: 3.75%–4.00%.** The Fed raised the range 25bp on September 16, 2026, by a 12–0 vote, its first hike since 2023. It held on October 28, 2026, by 10–2 (Fed press release, Oct 28, 2026; Fox Business, Sep 16, 2026).
- **What YES requires:** One 25bp hike, to 4.00%–4.25%, is enough because only the upper bound needs to reach 4.25%. A 50bp hike also qualifies.
- **Only one scheduled meeting is left:** December 8–9, 2026, with the decision on Wednesday, December 9 at about 2:00 PM ET. A new range normally takes effect the next day, December 10. The Fed's page updates on decision day, and the rules allow early resolution once the data is published.
- **Emergency moves count,** but an intermeeting hike is very unlikely. The last one was in April 1994, and recent intermeeting moves (2001, 2008, 2020) were all cuts.
- **Edge cases:**
  - Could the window include earlier dates? The upper bound was at or above 4.25% until September 2025. However, it was 3.75% when 2026 began (after the December 2025 cut to 3.50%–3.75%) and has not been above 4.00% at any point in 2026. If the window is read as 2026 only, there is no prior hit. If it were read as including 2025, it would already be YES. The question's framing ("reach… before 2027") suggests the forward-looking reading.
  - **What NO looks like:** The range is held at or below 3.75%–4.00% on December 9 and there is no emergency hike between December 10 and 31.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates

**A. September dot plot calling for more moves in the same year (2015–2025).** I reconstructed this from memory of past Fed projections, not by checking them.
- In the September projections of 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2022, 2023, 2024 and 2025, the median implied at least one more move before year-end.
- That move was delivered by December in 7 of 8 years. The miss was 2023, when the September median showed one more hike and the Fed held.
- **Base rate: about 85–90%.**
- In September 2026, the median year-end rate was 4.1% (a 4.00%–4.25% range, so exactly one more hike), and 16 of 18 participants projected at least one more hike in 2026 (FXStreet, Sep 16, 2026; Newsquawk).

**B. The Fed moving in a direction it has strongly signaled about three weeks before a meeting, with officials backing it publicly.**
- Since the late 1990s, the Fed has rarely surprised markets on the direction of a move in the final weeks before a meeting.
- **Exceptions mostly reduced the size of a move rather than cancelling it.** In March 2023, after the Silicon Valley Bank failure, a likely 50bp hike became 25bp. In June 2016, a possible hike was dropped after a weak jobs report, but it had not been the base case.
- **Base rate: well above 90%** when the move is the clear consensus and there is no shock. This is a qualitative judgment, not a counted tally.

**C. This Fed specifically (Chair Warsh, 2026).** This cuts the other way.
- In late September, a hike at the October meeting looked likely. That expectation faded within weeks after comments from New York Fed President Williams, and the Fed held (NST/Reuters, Oct 2026).
- Warsh says he does not believe in forward guidance outside a crisis, and he declined to signal December in October (CNBC, Oct 28, 2026). After that, the Fed's messaging was criticized as muddled (Reuters/McGeever via Devdiscourse).
- So this Fed has shown it will hold even when expectations lean toward a hike.

## 4. Drivers and scenarios

**Paths to YES (a 25bp or 50bp hike on Dec 9):**
- Core inflation keeps printing 0.3% a month, energy prices stay high, the labor market is firm, and activity is described as "strong."
- A broad group of officials backs a hike: hawks (Waller, Logan, Musalem, Schmid), gradualists (Hammack, Collins, Paulson, Barkin) and October dissenter Cook.
- The September dot plot already pointed to exactly this move.
- Political pressure eased after the midterms.
- Credibility costs of skipping again: the October hold drew criticism, so a second skip would be costly.

**Paths to NO (hold on Dec 9):**
- **Warsh leads a hold,** citing AI-driven productivity gains, tighter financial conditions from the roughly 5%+ 10-year yield, and a desire to wait for November CPI (out Dec 10). He shows no attachment to forward guidance and engineered an October hold.
- **A financial-market shock:** turmoil in Treasuries or credit, or a disorderly selloff.
- **A sharp energy-price reversal** after an Iran ceasefire or de-escalation, plus a soft November jobs report on Dec 4 (for example, negative payrolls or unemployment at 4.3% or higher).
- **Data disruption,** such as a government shutdown delaying releases, giving the committee cover to wait.
- **The likely shape of NO:** a hold in December with the hike pushed to January 2027. That would resolve NO here, since there are no further meetings in 2026.

## 5. Signposts

- **Warsh remarks (Nov 16 and any later):** explicit concern about inflation persistence would raise the probability. Emphasis on productivity, financial conditions, or "data in hand is limited" would lower it. **This is the largest single signpost.**
- **Williams (NY Fed) and Jefferson (Vice Chair) before the Nov 28 blackout:** their comments moved October expectations. Support for a December hike is a strong YES signal; "patience" language is a strong NO signal.
- **Pre-blackout leaks:** WSJ or Bloomberg articles in the final days of November reliably signal the Fed's intended decision.
- **Dec 4 jobs report:** strong lowers NO risk; very weak (negative payrolls, unemployment up 0.2 points or more) raises it materially.
- **October PCE (late Nov):** core PCE at 0.3% or more per month supports YES.
- **Oil and the Iran conflict:** a ceasefire and a collapse in oil prices would be a NO signal.
- **Bond markets:** a disorderly rise in long-term yields or credit stress could argue either for a hike (fighting inflation) or for a hold (stability).
- **Political interventions** or a shutdown affecting data.
