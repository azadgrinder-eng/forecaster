# Research brief: Will 2026 be the hottest year on record in NASA GISTEMP?

## 1. Resolution mechanics

- **What counts:** the unsmoothed annual (J‑D) value for 2026 in NASA GISS's Land‑Ocean Temperature Index (GISTEMP v4, anomaly against the 1951–1980 base). It must be strictly above both (a) the GISS 2025 value and (b) 1.28 °C.
- **Which condition binds:** NASA gives 2025 at about 1.19 °C (NASA Earth indicators / Carbon Brief, Jan 2026). So the 1.28 °C floor is the real hurdle. 1.28 °C was NASA's announced value for 2024, the current record.
- **Rounding:** GISS publishes to 0.01 °C. Because the rule says "above," a published 1.28 resolves NO. 2026 needs at least 1.29. That matters because several analysts now describe the likely outcome as a near tie with 2024 (Carbon Brief headline, ~Nov 12, 2026).
- **Revisions to 2024 don't matter:** the threshold is fixed at 1.28. If GISS later revises 2024 to 1.29 or 1.30, a 2026 value of 1.29 would still resolve YES by the letter of the rules, even though NASA might not call it a record. Watch for disputes where "record" in the title and the numeric rule disagree.
- **Timing:** the market closes Dec 31, 2026. It expires at 10:00 AM after the 2026 data release, or 10:00 AM ET on April 1, 2027, whichever comes first. GISS normally publishes the December and annual figures in mid‑January, with a NASA/NOAA announcement. The rules don't say what happens if GISS hasn't published by April 1, 2027, for example because of a federal shutdown or GISS budget and staffing disruption. That is a tail risk with ambiguous resolution.
- **Who decides:** the GISS data table is the source. The question is about GISS only, not Copernicus/ERA5, NOAA or Berkeley Earth, whose rankings can differ.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates

1. **How often a year sets a new GISS record.** From memory of the GISTEMP series (not re-verified this session), new annual records were set in roughly 15–17 of the 45 years from 1980 to 2024. Recent examples are 1998, 2005, 2010, 2014, 2015, 2016, 2020, 2023 and 2024. That is about 35%. This is a weak prior for a year already 11 months in.
2. **Years with a strong El Niño building during the year:**
   - 1997 set a GISS record at the time (the peak effect landed mostly in 1998).
   - 2015 set a record.
   - 2023 set a record.
   - 1982 did not, because of the El Chichón eruption.

   Roughly 3 of 4 strong or super El Niño onset years set records. Usually the following year (1998, 2016, 2024) warms even more, because global temperature lags Niño‑3.4 by about 3–4 months.
3. **Expert probabilities in past years at this point in the year.** Berkeley Earth's and Carbon Brief's late-year "chance of record" estimates have generally been well calibrated once only 1–2 months remain, with a typical annual-value error of ±0.03–0.05 °C. I'm working from memory here and could not verify the exact error figures this session.

## 3. Current state of the world (most recent first)

- **~Nov 20, 2026 — Berkeley Earth October update:** October 2026 was the warmest October on record, at 1.80 ± 0.12 °C above 1850–1900.
  - Berkeley Earth puts the chance 2026 is the warmest year in its own record at about 97% (up from 96% in September, 93% in August, 69% in July and 12% in June). It gives about 3% for 2nd place.
  - It says NASA GISS, HadCRUT and Berkeley Earth are each likely to have 2026 as their nominal warmest year, while ERA5 is likely to land slightly lower.
  - Land only: about 84% chance 2026 finishes 2nd behind 2024. The ocean average for January–October is already a record (Berkeley Earth October 2026 update, Nov 2026).
- **~Nov 12, 2026 — NOAA October report:** the global surface temperature was 1.43 °C above the 20th-century average. That is the highest departure for any month since 1850. NOAA says 2026 is very likely top‑5, most likely 2nd in NOAA's record. Carbon Brief calls NOAA's record status "too close to call" (NOAA NCEI Oct 2026; weather.com, Nov 12, 2026).
- **~Nov 12, 2026 — Carbon Brief quarterly State of the Climate:**
  - Average of six datasets: 67% chance 2026 beats 2024, with a projected ~1.52 °C above preindustrial.
  - **NASA GISTEMP specifically: 82%.** Berkeley Earth: 97%.
  - If this El Niño warms the globe no more than the strongest past events did, the odds fall to about 50%.
  - January–September was 2nd warmest, behind 2024, at about 1.45 °C. September was 2nd warmest, behind 2023. August set a record in all six datasets.
  - 2027 is very likely to set a new record (Carbon Brief, Nov 2026).
- **Nov 2026 — Yale Climate Connections:** NASA also ranked October 2026 as the warmest October. I could not get NASA's actual October number on the 1951–1980 base; the GISS data pages were unreachable.
- **Nov 11, 2026 — Copernicus/ERA5:** October was 1.75 °C above preindustrial, beating October 2023 by 0.05 °C.
  - ERA5 gives about 80% that 2026 finishes 2nd and about 20% that it ties or beats 2024. 2026 is virtually certain to exceed 1.5 °C.
  - The Niño‑3.4 monthly SST anomaly hit an all-time record of +3.21 °C (Copernicus, Nov 2026). Berkeley Earth notes ERA5 rates 2024 relatively warmer than other datasets do, so the ERA5 figure understates GISS's odds.
- **Nov 2026 — Phys.org/WMO-style composite:** January–September 2026 was 1.47 °C above 1850–1900, below 2024's 1.55 °C, with 2026 "set to be one of the 4 hottest." This is the more cautious framing.

  Rough arithmetic: to match 1.55 for the full year, October–December must average about 1.79. October came in at 1.75 (ERA5) and 1.80 (Berkeley Earth), so a tie or near tie is plausible on composite measures.
- **ENSO (Nov 12, 2026, via secondary sources):**
  - CPC reportedly kept its El Niño Advisory with Niño‑3.4 near +2.5 °C, and lowered the chance of a record-strength event to about 60% for October–December. I have not verified this against CPC directly.
  - The IRI weekly Niño‑3.4 was +3.3 °C around Oct 14. WMO expects the peak in November–December. All 27 IRI models show a "very strong" event through DJF 2026–27. Carbon Brief expects a peak near 4 °C in December, more than 1 °C above the prior record.
- **Apr 30, 2026 — Hansen (Columbia):** predicted 2026 would be the warmest year in GISS, about 0.06 °C above 2024. That was on the assumption of a 2023-sized El Niño; the actual event is much stronger.
- **Earlier record-odds trajectory:** Hausfather/Carbon Brief gave 7% in March, 19% in April and 35% in July/August. They have risen sharply with each month of data.
- **Scheduled before close and resolution:**
  - GISS November data: about mid-December 2026.
  - Copernicus November bulletin: about Dec 5–8.
  - Berkeley Earth November update and NOAA November report: mid-December.
  - GISS December and annual data: about mid-January 2027, after the market closes but before it expires.

## 4. Drivers and scenarios

**YES paths:**
- November and December GISS anomalies come in at record levels, plausibly near or above the hottest months ever recorded (e.g., about 1.4–1.5 °C on the GISS base), because the record El Niño's warming peaks with a lag. The annual value then reaches 1.29 or more.
- GISS's methodology helps. It interpolates over the Arctic and other sparse regions, which tends to favor recent warm years compared with ERA5 and NOAA. Carbon Brief's 82% for GISS versus 67% for the six-dataset average reflects that.

**NO paths:**
- The El Niño's effect on global temperature is no larger than in past strong events, and November–December are warm but not exceptional. The annual value lands at 1.25–1.28. A tie at exactly 1.28 is NO.
- Year-to-date 2026 has trailed 2024 (2nd for January–September), so the record depends on a strong finish.
- Smaller risks:
  - Data revisions or methodology changes.
  - Publication delays past April 1, 2027.
  - A dispute if GISS revises 2024 above 1.28 and NASA says 2026 "tied" or was "second."

## 5. Signposts

- **Copernicus November bulletin (~Dec 5–8):** a record November by at least 0.1 °C over November 2023 would push the odds up. A non-record November would push them down sharply.
- **GISS November value (~mid-Dec):** the most direct evidence. With it, compute what December must be: GISS year-to-date through November, plus December, must give an annual value of at least 1.29.
- **Berkeley Earth and Carbon Brief updated GISTEMP-specific odds:** Berkeley Earth's nominal ranking for GISS is especially useful.
- **Daily ERA5 data (Climate Pulse) through December:** sustained daily anomalies of about 1.7–1.8 °C or more above preindustrial point to YES. A drift back toward about 1.5 °C points to NO.
- **ENSO:** CPC's December 11 discussion and weekly Niño‑3.4. A continued peak near +3–4 °C supports YES. An earlier-than-expected peak or decline weakens it slightly, since effects lag anyway.
- **Rules risk:** any news of a GISS data-release delay, shutdown or program disruption.

## 6. Uncertainties

- I could not get the actual GISS monthly values for 2026 or the year-to-date versus 2024; the GISS data tables couldn't be fetched. So I can't state how far behind 2024's pace GISS is through October.
- I don't know whether GISS's current published 2024 value is still 1.28 or has been revised, for example to 1.29 or 1.30. That changes how a near tie would be described, though not the numeric threshold.
- The November CPC ENSO details (+2.5 °C, ~60% chance of record strength) come from secondary sites and are unverified.
- Estimates still differ by dataset: ERA5 ~20%, NOAA "too close to call," GISS 82%, Berkeley Earth 97%. The GISS-specific estimate comes from one modeler (Carbon Brief/Hausfather).
- The historical base rates in section 2 come from memory of the GISTEMP series, not re-verified this session.
- The rules don't say how the market resolves if GISS hasn't published by April 1, 2027.
