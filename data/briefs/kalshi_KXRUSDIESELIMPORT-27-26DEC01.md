# Research brief: Will a Source Agency report that Russian-origin diesel was imported into the U.S. before Dec 1, 2026?

*Prepared Nov 10, 2026. About 20 days remain before the close.*

## 1. Resolution mechanics

**What counts as YES.** Russian-origin diesel has to be physically delivered to a U.S. port, or otherwise into U.S. territory, for commercial or government use in the U.S. The delivery has to happen after "Issuance" and before Dec 1, 2026 (the market closes 04:59 UTC Dec 1). A Source Agency also has to report it.
- An import counts under any authority: the Oct 9 U.S.–Russia deal, OFAC General License 135, or anything else.
- "Russian-origin" means the diesel was produced in Russia. Diesel refined elsewhere from Russian crude does not count.

**What does not count:**
- Purchases, contracts, loadings or voyages that have not yet delivered.
- Diesel used as the ship's own fuel (bunkers).
- Fuel held in bond for re-export, or passing through the U.S. in transit.
- Announcements or licenses on their own.

**Ambiguities and edge cases:**
- **Which agencies count.** The text I received does not list the "Source Agencies." Forecasters should check the market page for the list. It probably includes major wire services and newspapers (Reuters, AP, Bloomberg and similar), all of which follow this story closely.
- **"Issuance."** This is probably the market's creation date. Reports that only repeat pre-Issuance coverage don't count.
- **U.S. territory.** "Otherwise into U.S. territory" probably includes Puerto Rico, the USVI and Guam. Puerto Rico bought Russian and Baltic products before 2022 but had nearly stopped by 2022 (S&P Global, Apr 2022). I found no 2026 reporting about the territories.
- **Partial or disputed discharges.** If a tanker arrives and CBP detains the cargo or refuses entry, it is unclear whether that is "physically delivered … for use within the U.S." Offloading into bonded storage would be excluded. The rules leave this gray zone open.
- **Origin laundering.** Ship-to-ship transfers or blending could hide the origin. Any import like that would probably go unreported in time, which pushes toward NO.
- **Who confirms.** The question turns on reporting, not on official customs data. EIA import data by country lags by about two months, so it will not be available before close.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates

**A. U.S. imports of Russian petroleum products since the 2022 ban.**
- The Ending Importation of Russian Oil Act (P.L. 117‑109, April 2022) bans HTS Chapter 27 products from Russia.
- Since the GL 17 wind-down ended in June 2022, I know of no confirmed U.S. imports of Russian-origin diesel. That makes about 0 of roughly 52 months (my general knowledge; not re-verified this session).
- Before the war, Russian diesel/distillate made up only a small share of U.S. imports. Fuel oil and VGO dominated.
- **Base rate in a normal month: about 0%.** This class leaves out the new license, so it is a floor.

**B. Politically toxic energy cargoes that are legally permitted.**
- **Russian-linked LNG, Jan 2018.** A Yamal-derived LNG cargo delivered to Everett, Massachusetts, was controversial but discharged.
- **Venezuelan crude under Chevron's GL 41.** The license was issued Nov 26, 2022, and the first cargo reached the U.S. about 5–6 weeks later. That trade had a willing, licensed buyer.
- **The 2022 GL 17 wind-down.** Cargoes already contracted kept arriving, although CBP held some until paperwork was checked (summarized by discoveryalert.com, Nov 2026).

When a committed buyer existed, the cargo usually landed within weeks. Here no buyer is confirmed, which makes the present case weaker.

**C. This deal so far: tankers announced as U.S.-bound that actually discharged.**
- 0 of 3–4 tracked tankers have discharged (Talisman, Leruo, Agioi Fanendes, and Beast, which is sanctioned).
- At least 2 diverted, to Algeria/the Mediterranean and to Brazil.
- About 32 days have passed since GL 135 with zero deliveries (discoveryalert.com; Al Jazeera, Nov 7, 2026).

## 3. Current state of the world (most recent first)

- **Nov 9, 2026.** Agioi Fanendes (Malta flag, Greek-owned by Eastern Mediterranean Maritime) was underway in the North Atlantic with no declared destination. It carries about 40,000 t loaded at Primorsk on Oct 22, and Reuters identified it as the first U.S.-bound cargo.
  - It nearly halved its speed and missed its Nov 7 New York ETA.
  - Earlier it was reported south of Newfoundland, still heading toward the U.S. East Coast.
  - The manager says the discharge port is "not confirmed yet" (United24 Media, Nov 9; Charter'97, Nov 9).
  - On Nov 9, AAA's national average diesel price was $6.17/gal, down from $6.29 a week earlier; September's record was $6.53 (Charter'97, Nov 9).
- **Nov 9.** Charter'97 headlined "Trump and Putin's diesel deal has fallen through." Lavrov, by contrast, says the deal "is working" and lowering prices (JPost).
- **Nov 7.** Al Jazeera reported "zero Russian diesel has reached the US … buyers fear legal action." CNN (Nov 7) also covered the stranded tanker.
- **Around Nov 5–6.** The Financial Times, Moscow Times, AP via PBS and France 24 reported that Talisman diverted toward Algeria ("for orders") and Leruo toward Brazil.
  - Kpler's Matt Smith said there was "little doubt" Agioi Fanendes would sell elsewhere, and that U.S. firms are reluctant because of legal and sanctions risk (bne IntelliNews; Moscow Times, Nov 5).
  - U.S. distillate stocks fell below 100 million barrels, the lowest in more than 20 years (Pravda.com.ua, Nov 5).
- **Early November (undated).** The Washington Sun reported that Trump officials are privately pressing U.S. firms to accept Russian diesel. The cargoes had no confirmed buyer when loaded.
- **Nov 3.** The U.S. midterms took place. The deal's pre-election political motive has now passed. House Ukraine Caucus Democrats asked CBP Commissioner Rodney Scott to refuse entry and refer any attempted import for prosecution (Doggett press release; TexasPolitics, Nov 3).
- **Late October.** New Jersey Gov. Mikie Sherrill warned port and terminal operators of multi-million-dollar fines.
  - On Oct 26, a bipartisan group of lawmakers, led by Sen. Shaheen, warned bank, port-authority and fuel-distributor CEOs against handling Russian diesel. Targets included New York, Houston, Corpus Christi and Beaumont (Senate Foreign Relations Committee release).
  - Ukraine said it was monitoring 8 tankers that might be bound for the U.S. (RFE/RL).
- **Oct 26.** Russian refiners raised diesel output after export quotas were granted (Reuters via US News).
- **Oct 23.** Reuters reported the first tanker heading to New York. The same day, MarineTraffic showed its destination changing to "for orders."
- **Oct 10.** Russia partially lifted the diesel export ban it had imposed in July 2026 after refinery damage.
- **Oct 9.**
  - Trump announced that Putin agreed to supply 300,000 t "immediately," 500,000 t in November, then 1 million t, to the U.S. and global markets (Al Jazeera, Oct 10; Fortune, Oct 10).
  - The same day, OFAC issued GL 135. It authorizes the sale, delivery, offloading and import of Russian-origin diesel until Apr 7, 2027, with no accompanying guidance (Clyde & Co; HSF Kramer).
  - GL 135 does not override the 2022 statute. Lifting that ban requires presidential certification of a ceasefire, among other conditions, followed by a 90-day congressional review (Lawfare; TIME, Oct 13).
  - No court has ruled, and no CBP directive has been found.

**Scheduled or possible events before Dec 1:**
- Possible further loadings of the promised "500,000 t in November." Primorsk to New York took about 16 days for Agioi Fanendes, so a cargo would need to load by about Nov 12–14 to arrive before Dec 1.
- Possible CBP guidance.
- Possible litigation.
- Winter heating demand in the Northeast.

## 4. Drivers and scenarios

**Paths to YES:**
1. **Agioi Fanendes finds a U.S. buyer.** It is already on the Atlantic side, about 2–4 days from the East Coast. This would most likely happen through administration pressure on a trader, or a politically aligned buyer, and a terminal willing to accept the cargo outside New Jersey.
   - It depends on a buyer accepting criminal and civil exposure under the 2022 Act. The statute of limitations is about 5 years ("five-year fuse," OilPrice), which outlasts the administration.
   - It also depends on CBP admitting the cargo.
2. **A government purchase.** For example, DOE or DLA could buy the cargo for government use. I found no evidence of this.
3. **A smaller or quieter delivery** to Puerto Rico, the Gulf Coast or a territory, which a wire service then reports.
4. **A new cargo** loaded in November that reaches the U.S. before Dec 1. The timing is tight, and the buyer problem is the same.

**Paths to NO (the current trajectory):**
- No buyer will take on the statutory risk, and the remaining tanker diverts to Brazil, Turkey, Morocco or West Africa. Kpler's base case is that it sells elsewhere.
- CBP detains or refuses the cargo, or it goes into bond.
- The administration lets the issue fade now that the midterms are over.

| Lawsuits or state injunctions, or more governors issuing warnings | Toward NO |
