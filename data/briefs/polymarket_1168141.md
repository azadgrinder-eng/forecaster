# Research Brief: Will the upper bound of the fed funds target be 4.25% at the end of 2026?

*Research cutoff: Nov 22, 2026. Web search worked, but every fetch of a full page (federalreserve.gov, CNBC and others) was refused by the network proxy. The facts below therefore come from search-result summaries of the cited articles, not from the primary texts. Where secondary sources disagreed, I say so. ## 1. Resolution mechanics

- **What counts:** the upper bound of the FOMC's target range after the Dec 8–9, 2026 meeting. The statement is expected Dec 9 at about 2:00 pm ET. If no December decision is issued by Dec 31, 2026 at 11:59 pm ET, the market uses whatever upper bound is in effect then. The source is the Federal Reserve's open-market page.
- **Current range:** 3.75%–4.00%, so the upper bound is **4.00%** (in place since the Sept 16, 2026 hike, held Oct 28). FRED showed a lower bound of 3.75% on Nov 20, and the effective rate was about 3.88% on Nov 19 (Fed Gazette, Nov 2026).
- **What YES needs:** a net increase of exactly +25 bp by year-end. In practice that means a 25 bp hike on Dec 9 to 4.00%–4.25%.
- **What gives NO:**
  - a hold (stays at 4.00%);
  - a 50 bp hike (4.50%);
  - any other net change, including an unscheduled move between meetings.
- **Edge cases:**
  - The rounding rule only matters for non-standard increments, which are very unlikely.
  - A meeting postponement is not a real risk: the Dec 31 fallback still uses whatever range is in effect.
  - Timing is not ambiguous. The market can resolve on Dec 9.

## 2. Reference classes and base rates

**A. Heavily signaled FOMC moves get delivered.** Since the Fed began announcing decisions (1994), and especially since 2009, it has almost never skipped a move that the minutes, Fed speakers and nearly all forecasters had lined up weeks ahead. These are from my own knowledge, not this session's searches:
- December hikes in 2015, 2016, 2017 and 2018 were all delivered as signaled.
- Dec 2018 went ahead despite an S&P 500 fall of roughly 15% and VIX readings in the 30s.
- The 2004–06 cycle had 17 straight signaled 25 bp hikes, all delivered.

Rough base rate: well above 90% that a move is delivered when the minutes say "almost all participants" expect it and every economist in a Reuters poll forecasts it.

Counterexamples are partial:
- March 2023: the SVB failure cut an expected 50 bp to 25 bp, but the Fed still hiked.
- Sept 2015 and early 2016: holds after market turmoil, but those hikes had not been firmly signaled.

**B. Size of moves in gradual hiking cycles.**
- 2004–06: 17 of 17 hikes were 25 bp.
- 2015–18: 9 of 9 were 25 bp.
- 2022–23: front-loaded with 50 and 75 bp moves, while the Fed was far behind inflation of 8–9%.
- The current cycle's only hike (Sept 2026) was 25 bp.

The closest analog for an upgrade to a bigger move is June 2022, when the Fed went 75 bp instead of the expected 50 bp. That followed a hot CPI print released days before the meeting. Here, November CPI comes out Dec 10, the day after the decision, so no CPI shock can land before the meeting.

**C. Hikes during sharp equity selloffs.**
- Dec 2018: hiked anyway.
- Sept 2015: held, with an August 2015 selloff cited.
- Early 2016: paused.
- March 2023: hiked through bank stress.

Rough split: when the hike was firmly signaled, the Fed went ahead in most cases (about 3 of 4). Pauses happened mainly when the hike was not yet locked in.

## 4. Drivers and scenarios

**YES (25 bp hike to 4.25%)** is the central case. It is supported by:
- the minutes saying "almost all" participants expect more hikes;
- the unanimous Reuters poll;
- two hawkish dissents already in October;
- core inflation still running at about 0.3% a month;
- a labor market that is holding up.

Warsh's gradualist, no-forward-guidance approach, with Williams and Hammack opposed to "dramatic action," points to 25 bp rather than 50 bp.

**NO via hold (4.00%).** The case rests on:
- the AI-led selloff deepening into a broader tightening of credit and funding conditions, or AI-debt or financial-stability stress (several participants flagged AI-related debt in the minutes);
- a weak November jobs report on Dec 4;
- political pressure after the midterms.

Working against it: the Fed held in October and then wrote in the minutes that December is likely. Reversing that again would hurt its credibility, and Cook says conditions are still too easy. A hold most likely requires a disorderly market event, not just a 10% correction.

**NO via 50 bp (4.50%).** The case rests on:
- Waller's push, plus the "couple" of participants in the minutes;
- a hot November jobs report or October PCE;
- the argument that the October pause left the Fed behind the curve.

Working against it: Waller appears isolated; Williams, Hammack and Paulson favor gradual moves; the selloff and falling 2-year yields argue against acceleration; and November CPI arrives after the meeting.

**NO via an unscheduled move** (between meetings or emergency) is very low probability.

## 5. Signposts

- **Dec 4 jobs report:**
  - Strong (above about 150k and unemployment at or below 4.1%) adds to 50 bp risk, but mainly confirms 25 bp.
  - Very weak (negative payrolls, or unemployment at or above 4.4%) raises hold risk.
- **October PCE / core PCE, if released before Dec 9:** a core reading at or above 0.35% m/m would boost the 50 bp case.
- **Equity and credit stress between now and Nov 27:**
  - VIX staying above 35, credit spreads widening, or problems at an AI-linked lender or fund would raise hold odds.
  - Stabilization keeps 25 bp as the base case.
- **Fed speeches before the blackout (about Nov 28):**
  - Watch Warsh, Jefferson and Williams. Any hint of "patience" or "assessing financial conditions" points to a hold.
  - More officials joining Waller's call points to 50 bp.
- **Any intentional media signal during the blackout**, such as a WSJ or Nick Timiraos-type story. Historically these accurately preview the size of a move (for example, June 2022).
